Let's start with a premise. The things produced by human labor fall into two categories. Things that keep moving even after the creator is gone—buildings, code, recipes, systems. And things that are useful in the moment and vanish in the moment—customer service, coordination, verification, meetings. Both have value, but their natures are different. The things that remain work through the night; the things that vanish stop the instant you stop your hands.

And the world's accounting pays these two types in different currencies. For vanishing work, there is a salary—you get paid for as much as you work, and it stops when you stop. For things that remain, there is the owner's share—as long as it exists, money comes in even while you sleep. This isn't some conspiracy; it is the structure of bookkeeping. A conversation about annual income and a conversation about assets are two different conversations.

When you layer this over the only resource you possess from the start—time—ways of living split into three. The difference lies in where you place the thing your time has been converted into.

Storing it outside yourself. Created objects, systems, trust. These keep moving even if you stop, and they benefit from compound interest. In exchange, you shoulder the heaviest risk: it doesn't become income until it solidifies.

Storing it inside yourself. Skills that are recognized anywhere. The ability to carry them with you becomes your insurance—to be employable anywhere means you can leave from anywhere. However, because it requires your own time to withdraw that value every single time, your working hours become the ceiling for your income.

Not storing it at all. In exchange for letting go of compound interest, you receive the full value in the present. You exercise sovereignty over your time right now. Freelancers or part-time workers are legitimate residents of this path, and that too is fine.

All three are correct. There is only one kind of failure: a mismatch between the path you are on and the reward you are waiting for. And the worst mismatch is "storing it in someone else's vessel." This means pouring years into skills that only work within a specific company. Because it isn't inside you, you can't take it with you; and because it isn't your own thing outside yourself, you don't own it. You are piling things up in a place that is none of the three storage locations.

Try writing down three of your skills and erasing the company name. What remains after erasing is the skill you can take with you. What disappears is the savings in someone else's vessel. Being unable to write anything isn't a lack of effort; it's simply that the piggy bank wasn't yours. Starting tomorrow, you can choose to learn things that remain even after the company name is erased.

There is no such thing as a "loser" on any of these paths. There is only the misunderstanding of which path you are on.
