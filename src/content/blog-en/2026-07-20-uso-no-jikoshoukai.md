Companies say, "We can do it all." They write as if growth, stability, fulfillment, and work-life balance are all present. Applicants say, "I'll do anything." They erase their strengths and weaknesses to play the part of an infinitely flexible resource.

In other words, both sides are erasing their own information to increase the probability of a match. The more information you strip from your self-introduction, the easier it becomes to get a job offer. And since two people devoid of information are shaking hands, a bad match isn't an accident—it's inevitable. It is the handshake of one empty box with another.

Furthermore, "interview prep" is simply practice for this lie. Polishing your answers to anticipated questions means shaving down your own irregularities to fit the shape of the other party's mold. The system itself teaches misrepresentation. Even though no one is a "bad person," an equilibrium has been established where everyone gives a fake self-introduction. You can look for a villain, but you won't find one. You simply have to accept that this is how the mechanism works.

As an experiment, try drawing lines on a job posting you have on hand. Mark the "fact lines" (salary, location, specific nouns regarding job duties) and the "promise lines" ("you can grow," "open communication," "young employees thrive"). Next, draw the same lines through the things you said during your interview. Which side had more lines?

![Drawing lines for fact lines and promise lines on a job posting](/img/pipes/uso-no-jikoshoukai.png)

A correct self-introduction isn't a matter of how much you embellish. It's a matter of the materials used. You cannot write it with desires; you can only write it with records. From now on, for both the other party's documents and your own words, you only need to read the fact lines.
