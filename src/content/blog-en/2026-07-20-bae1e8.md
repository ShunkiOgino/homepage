"Finally, I want to make a game." I believe that in the moment those words were spoken, that child was saved for the first time.

Recently, in the fields of engineering and business building, I've seen many people break because they clung too tightly to the "correct answer." From my own observations, salvation in stories often takes a similar form: an escape from "obsession." For example, the norm that "one must not hate others," or the bravado of "I must not lose." People cling to these "absolutes" within themselves, and when those absolutes crumble, they break. The basic engine of a story is that through this collapse, the person is saved by being recognized as an individual rather than a role.

I myself had wandered into a maze of "how can I reach the correct answer" regarding the design of my current product, but rereading this story reminded me once again of the strength found in letting go of that obsession with correctness.

However, the character Subaru from the novel *(Insert Work Name)* was an anomaly. Because he existed in a state of "non-attachment," possessing almost no obsession even with himself, he didn't enter this cycle of collapse at all. While those around him were exhausted by clashes of values, he maintained an inexplicable strength, existing there as a "mysteriously strong" presence.

For him, salvation functioned in the opposite vector. A person who wanted nothing finally felt a first-time desire: "I want to make a game." By choosing to have an obsession, he who had none gave his life a contour, and thus salvation was achieved.

I also want to consider the conclusion of this story. What was chosen here was not the "best" route—where a disease is miraculously cured—but rather a "next-best" form of salvation based on the acceptance of death. The reason his best friend, Leon, didn't consult him sooner despite realizing the truth was nothing other than a craving to "not let it end."

Depicting the weak as weak and the flawed as flawed. By choosing the next-best ending instead of escaping to a "correct answer" like efficiency or a happy ending, the integrity of the story is preserved.

Strength is sometimes established by "having nothing." But I believe that true salvation occurs the moment a small obsession—a "this is what I want"—lights up within that void.

What is the true nature of the "strength" supporting you right now?
