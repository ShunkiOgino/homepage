Every day, I post a short video recording how much I worked that day. From July 14th to the 17th, these were getting between 1,097 and 1,862 views per video. However, from the 18th onward, they plummeted to 276, 163, and 144. Something that normally averaged over 1,000 views had dropped to around 200.

My first thought was, "People are bored of this." Right around that time, I had changed the format of the videos. I added a step-count graph and revamped the presentation. The timing overlapped perfectly. Therefore, I assumed that was the cause. I came dangerously close to just reverting the format back to how it was.

But when I looked at the numbers, it was a different story.

First: the engagement rate per viewer had hardly changed before and after the drop. In both cases, it was just fluctuating within a range of less than 1%. If people were bored, their reactions should have dulled. What had decreased wasn't the engagement, but the number of times the video was seen in the first place.

The second point hit harder. I lined up the "percentage of viewers who remained after the first few seconds" day by day. It turned out that the video from July 15th performed the best. The retention rate was over 60%, which is average compared to similar videos in the world—and the highest number among everything I've posted. Yet, that day's views were 1,097, the lowest of the four days. Conversely, the video from the 14th had a mediocre initial retention of about 40%, yet it grew to 1,855 views.

The quality of the hook did not explain the view count.

In other words, the period when views were growing wasn't because I was "making them well." It was simply that they happened to be distributed widely. In fact, looking at the channel as a whole, there are days where views jump by several thousand in a single day—3,130 on the 17th and 3,690 on the 19th. I had been riding a wave. And from the 18th onward, that wave receded.

This changed my judgment. **I will not revert the format.** If the cause of the drop isn't the format, reverting it won't fix anything; it would only mean breaking something that didn't need fixing.

This was the biggest lesson I learned this time. If I hadn't looked at the numbers, I would have undoubtedly reverted the format—simply because the timing overlapped. Then, I would have been left with the result that "I reverted it but it didn't fix anything," and I likely would have started tinkering with an even more irrelevant part of the process next.

Human intuition tends to assume that things happening close in time are causally linked. I think "measuring" is about preparing a state where you can produce counter-evidence against those assumptions. You could call it a system for not trusting your own gut.

To be honest, there is a hole in this analysis. The videos from the 18th onward haven't been out long, so the data for initial retention isn't fully complete yet. Therefore, the possibility that "the hook of the new format is weak" hasn't been completely eliminated. In fact, the video from the 18th has the lowest initial retention of the data I currently have. However, even so, it wasn't a gap large enough to explain a five- or ten-fold difference in views.

What I've figured out is that "it's not the format's fault," but I still don't know "what needs to be strengthened to ride the wave." For that, I'll wait for the next set of numbers.

(All numbers used here are as of July 24, 2026. Since view counts are sometimes revised downward later, the values on the [Analysis](/analysis) page may be slightly different if you look now. I leave both the good and bad numbers as they are, so feel free to look at the same data and reach a different conclusion.)
