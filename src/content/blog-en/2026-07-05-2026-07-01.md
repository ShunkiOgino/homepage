When I tried to solve scope management—who is allowed to see what—with add-on features, the mental load on the input side increased, and as a result, output stopped. Information dried up. From this experience, I have come to realize the importance of solving management through "structure" rather than "features."

When turning a flow (conversation) into a stock (record), you might think it's enough to set permissions after saving or to prompt the inputter to "be mindful of confidential information." However, when humans are forced to pay the decision cost of "what should the scope of this information be" at the exact moment of input, they unconsciously suppress their output. A design that relies on self-awareness becomes a wall that stops the inflow of information.

That is why I have adopted control based on a deterministic structure rooted in provenance—"which path did this enter from?" This is an approach to bring the input cost as close to zero as possible.

Particularly effective was the introduction of "rejection-based" operations for the "necessary evil" that is approval work. For example, when there are 56 pending approvals, instead of checking them one by one, I simplified the process so that AI pre-checks items it deems safe, and the human only needs to "uncheck the NG ones"—reducing it to a two-click operation. This minimized cognitive load, cleared the backlog of pending approvals that had been a bottleneck, and caused the speed of information circulation to skyrocket.

I apply this same principle to other designs. In community flow management, rather than exposing a user to a public space immediately upon entry, I use a flow where they are welcomed into a private room and joined to the main group only when an administrator assigns a role; the flow itself functions as permission management. In the design of AI workers, I adopted an integrated model of "one person = one agent" in the underlying logic layer and passed them through a filter that structurally blocks the injection of private memories into public channels, achieving both convenience and confidentiality. Furthermore, in knowledge management, I placed approval gates only for promotions from the L-layer to the V-layer and controlled it with a "24-hour revocable window" structure. This eliminated the time spent agonizing over individual posts and allowed me to focus on source quality control, automatically deploying it across five dimensions.

This kind of design philosophy does not work when introduced as an add-on tool. When you try to retroactively apply rationality to an existing organization, you inevitably encounter internal resistance and revert to the original state of ambiguity. This is not a product that can be installed; it is a design philosophy that must be carved into the DNA on Day 0.

Do not reduce it to a matter of will or ethics—"who should do what"—but let the path and structure determine the result. By doing so, you completely erase the "time spent hesitating" for the inputter, allowing them to continue creating value sustainably.

Could it be that the "heaviness" you feel right now is simply because you are being forced to pay a cost structurally?
