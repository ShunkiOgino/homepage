A "cliff" was occurring where viewers plummeted at the 5-second mark. Checking the retention curve, it stayed full for the first 4 seconds, but then a sudden mass exodus occurred.

Normally, one would assume this is an issue of content or presentation—perhaps the hook was too weak, or there was a disconnect between the title and the actual content. However, after analyzing the mp4 frames one by one, I discovered that the culprit wasn't the hook or the title, but rather a physical design flaw in the "rendering structure."

Specifically, the structure displayed an entire sentence of subtitles at the 0-second mark, and the same static screen persisted for about 7.5 seconds per card (one long sentence). Viewers finish reading that text within 3 to 4 seconds. However, because the audio continues to read the same sentence, a "blank space of time where no new information enters" is created between the 4th and 7th seconds.

During this dead time, viewers intuitively decide that they've "had enough" and drop off. In other words, the cliff in retention was a result of the viewer detecting a "thinness of information" before they even processed the actual content.

What we can derive from this is the necessity of introducing a visual change within 2 seconds. However, the limiting factor isn't simply the number of seconds. The critical point is whether the inequality "Interval of new information input ≤ Time it takes for the viewer to finish reading" holds true. By keeping this gap small, you can deliver information without breaking the viewer's focus.

I believe this is a principle common to all information design, not just video. No matter how valuable or correct your argument is, if a blank space is created where the next stimulus doesn't arrive "after the recipient has finished processing," the connection is severed. It is not about the correctness of the value, but about synchronizing the "speed" and "density" of the information being supplied. This is the minimum requirement for maintaining engagement.

In the materials or communications you are creating right now, where are the moments where you are "making the other person wait" after they have finished reading?
