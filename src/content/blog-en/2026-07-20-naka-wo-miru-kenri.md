The world is divided into the consuming side and the producing side, with a wall standing between them. From the buyer's perspective, you can see the price and the packaging, but you cannot see how it was made, who decided what, or where the money flows. The inner workings of a company are known only to those who have entered. Employment is almost the only publicly open gate for crossing this wall.

In other words, while getting a job looks like a "situation where you sell your labor," it actually contains a simultaneous reverse transaction. You are paying with your labor as an admission fee to buy a ticket into the world of those who create.
The reason job postings don't describe the inner workings isn't out of malice; it's because there is no way to describe them from the outside.

This reframing has a practical consequence. The selection process is not a "place where you are bought." You are buying, too. Therefore, you are allowed to have the eye of a buyer—asking yourself: "What will this position allow me to see on the inside?"

And the only thing that remains from this purchase is not your salary or your job title, but the understanding that settles in your mind. Salary is necessary to keep life running, but it disappears once spent. Only understanding can be taken with you. However, there is a saturation point to learning. What you can learn by observing increases sharply in the first one to two years, after which it becomes a repetition of the same scenery.

The signs of saturation also manifest in your actions. How many tasks have you performed for the first time in the last month? If there are almost none, it doesn't mean your growth has stopped; it means you have extracted everything you could from that seat—a state that, in school, would be called graduation. The more things you can answer instantly for your juniors, the closer your learning is to an end.

Getting a job is a purchase. You should buy based on the contents. And you are free to decide the price and the deadline for yourself.
