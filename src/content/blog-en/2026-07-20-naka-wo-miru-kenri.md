The world is divided into the consuming side and the producing side, with a wall standing between them. From the buyer's perspective, you can see the price and the packaging, but you cannot see how it is made, who decides what, or where the money flows. The inner workings of a company are known only to those who have entered. Employment is almost the only publicly open gate for crossing this wall.

In other words, while employment looks like a "scenario where you sell your labor," it actually contains a simultaneous reverse transaction. You are paying an admission fee in the form of labor to buy a ticket into the world of the producers. The reason job postings don't describe the inner workings isn't out of malice; it's because there is no way to describe them from the outside.

This reframing has a practical consequence. The selection process is not a place where you are "being bought." You are buying, too. Therefore, you are allowed to have the eye of a buyer—asking yourself: "What will this seat allow me to see inside?"

And the only thing that remains from this purchase is not your salary or your job title, but the understanding that settles in your mind. Salary is necessary to keep life running, but it disappears once spent. Only understanding can be taken with you. However, there is a saturation point to learning. What you can learn by observing increases sharply in the first one to two years, after which it becomes a repetition of the same scenery.

You can identify the signs of saturation through records. Open your notes from your first year and see if you can name "three things I learned anew this month." If you can't, it doesn't mean your growth has stopped; it means you have extracted everything you could from that seat—a state that, in school, would be called graduation. The more things you can answer instantly for your juniors, the closer your learning is to its end.

I am not saying don't get a job. I'm saying it is a purchase. You should buy after looking at the contents. And you are free to decide the price and the deadline yourself.
