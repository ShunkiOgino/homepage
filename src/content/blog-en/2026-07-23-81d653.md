The result of my drive for efficiency was a desperate cognitive load: having to identify the correct answer from 50 pieces of garbage. This happened because I shortened a one-hour information extraction process to a few seconds and increased the number of generated drafts to 50 at once.

At first, I thought that if I were automating with AI, maximizing the output and increasing the number of options would allow me to pick out the absolute best answer. I believed that as processing speed increased, work would naturally get faster. However, I was thinking only in terms of "technical skill" and "computational volume," completely ignoring the resources of the human actually making the judgment.

Increasing the amount of output directly increases the "amount that a human must judge." While processing speed jumped to a few seconds, the human bottleneck—the "sorting" process—bloated. As a result, the speed of decision-making actually dropped.

So, I gave up on "increasing output volume." Instead, I changed the design to give the LLM multi-layered evaluation criteria—such as "consistency with facts" and "suitability to context"—to narrow down the candidates for human judgment to just three. The processing speed itself dropped slightly, but the time it took for a human to decide "this is it" decreased dramatically.

The true cost of automation lies not in the machine's computation time, but in human cognitive resources. When running the system itself becomes the goal, you fall into the contradiction of consuming resources to continuously mass-produce low-quality output.

What we should be thinking about here is not "widening the pipe," but "refining the filter." There is no point in flowing a massive amount of water through the inlet if it takes forever to separate the mud at the outlet. By passing data through multi-layered evaluation gates, you create a state where only the "final three choices" reach the human. The value of automation is not in mass production, but in liberating humans from "hesitation" and maximizing the speed of decision-making.

What is it that you are increasing through automation right now? Is it "results," or is it the amount of "garbage" that needs to be processed?
