Wishing for an AI to be "honest" is a prayer, not a design. Last time I touched upon the design philosophy; this time, I will show you the internal implementation.

Usually, people think like this: if you don't want an AI to leak confidential information, you should establish powerful ethical guardrails or strictly command it via prompts to "absolutely keep secrets." In other words, they try to entrust security to the "will" or "honesty" of the AI as an individual entity. However, that is a highly costly and uncertain gamble.

I take the opposite approach. It doesn't matter how honest or dishonest the AI is.

In the AI workers I actually operate within my community, I keep the interface thin—a mere "window"—and complete all control within the logic layer behind it. I employ an integrated "one person = one agent" model that maps individuals from user IDs, and enable consistent responses across all channels by dynamically injecting memories for each counterpart—specifically "person cards" and "rolling summaries every six exchanges."

At the same time, I strictly limit permissions. I've introduced "write scopes" that restrict writing ranges based on roles, and applied OS-level sandboxing to arbitrary code execution tools to block access to confidential information. Furthermore, to achieve "approval-less" operation without requiring human sign-off every time, I have implemented structural guardrails such as blocking replies to its own posts and setting rate limits on the total number of daily replies and replies to a single counterpart (specific values are undisclosed but set based on operational standards).

Particularly important is the design that blocks the injection of private memories entirely in public channels and passes the output through a safety filter immediately before delivery. By doing this, I create a state where data is structurally unreachable even if the AI attempts to output information by mistake. Even in the event of contaminated external input or an AI runaway, this prevents a total system takeover or information leakage.

The key point here is the idea of replacing "trust" with "structural isolation."

If there is no path, one cannot walk. I create a state where the access path to the data physically does not exist, regardless of how honestly the AI tries to behave. Rather than continuing to pay monitoring costs by hoping for the AI's honesty, I design physical constraints from the start: "you can only touch this" and "you can only output up to this amount." I separate the interface from the permission layer and inject only the necessary memories on a case-by-case basis. This structural isolation is the low-cost security that enables sustainable coexistence with AI.

What are you asking of your AI? An elaborate "honesty," or a "structural safety" where no damage occurs even if it breaks?
