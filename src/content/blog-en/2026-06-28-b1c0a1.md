Wishing for an AI to be "honest" is a prayer, not a design. Last time I touched upon design philosophy; this time, I will show the internal implementation.

Normally, people think like this: if you don't want an AI to leak confidential information, you should establish powerful ethical guardrails or strictly command it via prompts to "absolutely keep secrets." In other words, they try to entrust safety to the "will" or "honesty" of the AI as an individual entity. However, that is a highly costly and uncertain gamble.

I take the opposite approach. It does not matter how honest or dishonest the AI is.

In the AI workers I actually operate within my community, I keep the interface thin—a mere "window"—and complete all control within the logic layer behind it. I employ an integrated model of "one person = one agent" that maps individuals via user IDs, enabling consistent responses across any channel by dynamically injecting memories for each counterpart—specifically "counterpart cards" and "rolling summaries every six exchanges."

At the same time, I strictly limit permissions. I have implemented "write scopes" that restrict writing ranges based on roles, and applied OS-level sandboxing to arbitrary code execution tools to block access to confidential information. Furthermore, to achieve "approval-less" operation—removing the need for human approval every time—I have laid down structural guardrails such as blocking replies to its own posts and implementing rate limits on the total number of daily replies and replies to a single counterpart (specific values are undisclosed but set based on operational standards).

Particularly important is the design that blocks the injection of private memories entirely in public channels and passes outputs through a safety filter immediately before delivery. By doing this, I create a state where data is structurally unreachable, even if the AI mistakenly attempts to output information. Even in the event of contaminated external input or an AI runaway, this prevents system-wide hijacking or information leakage.

The key point here is the idea of replacing "trust" with "structural isolation."

If there is no path, one cannot walk. I create a state where the physical path to the data does not exist, regardless of how honestly the AI tries to behave. Rather than continuing to pay monitoring costs in hopes of the AI's honesty, I design physical constraints from the start: "you can only touch this" and "you can only output up to this amount." I separate the interface from the permission layer and inject only the necessary memories on a case-by-case basis. This structural isolation is the low-cost security that enables sustainable coexistence with AI.

What are you seeking from your AI? An elaborate "honesty," or a "structural safety" where no damage occurs even if it breaks?
